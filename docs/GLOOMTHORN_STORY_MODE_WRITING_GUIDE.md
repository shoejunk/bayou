# Writing Story Mode for new players

The tutorial is a game adaptation of Gloomthorn. Its first job is to make
players care and help them learn to play. Use the novels for characters,
relationships, danger, and emotional stakes. Change or leave out source
material when that makes the game story clearer.

## Audience and voice

Write for a sixth-grade reader who knows neither the novels nor strategy
games. Use common words, short sentences, and clear subjects. A simple
sentence can still be tense, funny, sad, or surprising.

- Begin with a person in danger, a goal, or a hard choice.
- Show what happens, why it happens, and what the heroes do next.
- Introduce a person by their role before asking the player to remember a name.
- Give each scene one main problem. Keep most spoken panels under 45 words.
- Reuse a known character when they can carry a new helper's role. A name,
  magic device, or rule must earn its place through an immediate choice.
- Let players use a familiar team in their first independent battle. Save
  full card demonstrations for optional practice after the story ending.
- Aim for two to six short panels for a story scene. Add a panel when a
  necessary cause, choice, or outcome needs room. Teach a complex rule in
  several small steps rather than one dense speech.
- Let characters sound different through their choices and tone, not obscure
  words. Keep jokes brief and easy to understand.
- Avoid idioms, stacked metaphors, unexplained lore terms, and wordplay that
  loses its meaning in translation.
- Replace accounting, trials, paperwork, and research scenes with the
  chase, rescue, betrayal, escape, or confrontation they cause. Keep a detail
  only when it changes the player's understanding of the current danger.

## Teaching real play

Use the exact printed card, action, and ability names when the player needs
to select them. Define a new rule in plain language at the moment it matters.
Say who acts, which action to select, and where to aim. Explain the result
briefly. Corrections should tell the player how to recover.

Preserve mouse and keyboard instructions. Explain what the game highlights
and what the player must keep alive. Say clearly when a practice battle is
optional and when a normal rule allows more choices than a guided lesson.
Put optional drill instructions after Start Practice so the skip path remains
short. Do not make a later required review punish an earlier skip.

A piece leaving the board means it cannot keep fighting. Never announce an
avoidable character death as the reward for obeying a basic tutorial step.
Show lasting harm through care and recovery when that better serves the lesson.

Every required input must be an existing game command. Units always use
their real card definitions. Story props and character choices are not
extra game mechanics. Never invent an interaction, status, damage source,
or reward to force a story event onto the board.

## Revision loop

1. Export the actual active scenes and instructions from the compiled catalog.
2. Have independent writers revise separate paths without sharing drafts.
3. Give fresh critics the current player text without the writers' reasoning,
   earlier versions, or other critics' findings.
4. Ask critics to name the exact confusing line, unclear motive, dull scene,
   unexplained term, or missing action. Treat their response as editorial
   feedback, not as evidence of testing with real children.
5. Fix the findings and repeat blind review after meaningful changes.
6. Run the tutorial suite and render the changed screens at the minimum
   supported size. Keep gameplay, progress, roster, and action checks intact.

Sentence counts and word limits can catch dense writing. They cannot prove
that a scene is understandable or fun. Human playtesting remains useful.
